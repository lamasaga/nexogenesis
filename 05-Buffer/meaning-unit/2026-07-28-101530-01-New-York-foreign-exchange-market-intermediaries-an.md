### 核心表达

　　The New York foreign exchange market consists of all buyers and sellers of exchange on foreign centers, including commercial and industrial organizations and three classes of middlemen: banks, exchange dealers, and independent brokers. New York practically absorbs all American foreign exchange because international trade balances are adjusted by New York financiers. The stock operations of a foreign exchange department reduce to maintaining deposit balances abroad and matching inflows and outflows across demand drafts, cables, and long bills.

### 依据与细节

　　**Definition of the market:** "A market may be defined as the coming together of buyers and sellers." A New Haven bank buying from or selling to the New York London-exchange market is part of that market.

　　**Three classes of middlemen:**
1. **Banks with regular foreign exchange business:** buy bills from sellers and sell their own drafts on foreign correspondents. Includes specialized foreign exchange banks and ordinary commercial banks such as United States National Banks.
2. **Exchange dealers:** principal business is buying commercial and bankers' bills and reselling them, chiefly to banks.
3. **Independent brokers:** earn small commissions by bringing buyers and sellers together; they do not invest their own capital or buy bills themselves.

　　**New York dominance:**
- New York brokers furnish Western clients (directly or through local representatives) with daily market quotations and advise them promptly of intraday fluctuations.
- Telegraphic interruption causes a "practical standstill" of exchange transactions elsewhere because the West operates in the "dark" without New York quotes.
- "The absorptive power of the New York market, to digest not only the surplus foreign exchange of the Chicago market, but that of the entire United States as well, has been demonstrated for many years."

　　**Stock operations:**
- **Selling demand against demand.** A banker buys John Smith & Co.'s sight draft for £1,000 on London at 4.86 ($4,860), sends it to London for credit, then draws his own £1,000 sight draft on the new balance and sells it at 4.87 ($4,870), grossing **$10**. The author notes this is illustrative rather than the main profit source.
- **Selling cables against demand exchange.** A **cable** differs from a sight draft only in that the paying banker abroad is instructed by telegraphic message rather than by paper. Dealers keep adequate balances abroad replenished by continuous remittances of demand exchange.
- **Selling demand bills against remittances of long bills.** The dominant stock operation: bankers sell demand drafts against incoming commercial and bankers' long paper. Commercial long bills make up the bulk of foreign exchange traded. Buying and remitting long bills requires judgment beyond the exchange rate: credit of drawer and drawee, salability of the merchandise described in the bill of lading, and other factors must be weighed.

### 限制与边界

　　原文未提及： the exact mechanism by which New York quotations were transmitted to the West; the share of each intermediary class in total volume; typical spreads or profit margins on cable transactions; how dealers hedge maturity mismatches; the proportion of long bills versus demand bills in daily trading; settlement cycles between correspondent banks.
