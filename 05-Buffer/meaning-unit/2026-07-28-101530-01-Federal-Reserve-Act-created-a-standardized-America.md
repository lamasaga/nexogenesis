### 核心表达

　　Before the Federal Reserve Act, the United States lacked a true money market; the nearest substitute was New York's call-loan market, which more often reflected stock-market movements than general money conditions. The Act authorized national banks to accept drafts growing out of import/export transactions, creating a standardized, low-credit-risk instrument—the bank acceptance—and a visible discount rate that could mark the level of the money market.

### 依据与细节

　　The author calls the establishment of the American acceptance market "probably the most important effect at this time [1915] of the Federal Reserve Act." Key operational example:
- Jones, a New York coffee importer, arranges with his bank to finance a Rio purchase.
- Smith, the Brazilian grower, ships coffee and draws a ninety days' sight draft on the New York bank.
- Smith sells the draft to a Brazilian bank, which sends it to New York.
- The New York bank accepts the draft by writing "Accepted" across it and signing; the draft becomes the bank's primary obligation.
- Jones remains obligated to fund the bank, but if he defaults the bank must still pay.

　　Because the credit risk is "practically eliminated," acceptances became attractive secondary reserves—"almost as available as cash." A London banker is quoted as saying they let the banker "eat his cake and have it too" because the bank earns discount while retaining liquidity.

　　Price observations:
- Ninety days' sight acceptances in New York sold at one time as low as **2 per cent per annum**;
- Prime acceptances then commanded **2-3/8 per cent**.

　　A broker market developed: brokers made daily rounds of banks with lists of acceptances for sale and purchase, gathering rate information.

### 限制与边界

　　原文未提及： the legal reserve treatment of acceptances under the Federal Reserve Act; the total outstanding volume of acceptances in 1915; whether all national banks or only certain classes could accept; the maturities permitted beyond ninety days.
