### 核心表达

　　Because nearly 40 per cent of United States exports consisted of cotton, the way cotton was financed abroad created a disproportionate credit-risk exposure for American exchange buyers. The dominant practice—foreign buyers opening sterling credits through London bankers that were then turned over to often irresponsible American selling agents—produced forged bills of lading, unauthorized drafts, and losses running into millions of dollars.

### 依据与细节

　　Two financing methods coexisted:
- **Documentary payment bills** drawn on cotton merchants or spinners at sixty or ninety days' sight, handled like flour bills. Only "a small percentage" of cotton moved this way.
- **Banker-credit financing** (the dominant flow): the foreign buyer arranged with American firms to cable offers; accepted offers were confirmed with the names of foreign bankers on whom drafts were to be drawn. The American shipper then sent cotton under bills of lading "drawn to the shipper's order and endorsed in blank," attached drafts on the designated foreign banker, and sold the exchange in the market or forwarded it to New York.

　　The structural weakness: American exchange buyers could not control whose bills entered the market. When European buyers dealt with "second- or third-rate houses, or houses with no standing whatever," prime-European-bank acceptances still came into circulation because the banker's name masked the underlying shipper risk. Exchange buyers tried to limit exposure by keeping their "water line" on weak names low and relying on personal honesty rather than capital, but they still faced:
- **Forged documents** not representing actual goods;
- **Exchange drawn without authority**;
- Collateral (cotton) accepted "entirely without margin."

　　A reform effective **September 1, 1910**, introduced **validation certificates** printed on specially protected water-mark paper and attached to bills of lading in a way that made removal practically impossible. Bills of lading were no longer to be released until goods were actually in railroad possession, reducing the scope for shippers to fill in and obtain signatures for non-existent cargo.

### 限制与边界

　　原文未提及： the exact aggregate loss figure in dollars; whether the 1910 reform eliminated fraud or merely reduced it; the legal enforcement mechanism behind validation certificates; how frequently forged documents occurred relative to legitimate bills.

### 原文摘录

> "As nearly 40 per cent. of our exports consist of cotton, the method under which it is financed is worthy of special consideration."
> "Various abuses have developed under this system, which have caused losses running into millions of dollars to all of the various parties engaged in carrying the transactions to their close."
> "This system, which calls for validation certificates, numbered and printed upon a specially protected water-mark paper ... went into effect September 1, 1910."
