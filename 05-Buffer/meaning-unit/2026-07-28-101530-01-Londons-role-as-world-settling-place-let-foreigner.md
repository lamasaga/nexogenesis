### 核心表达

　　The United Kingdom accepted many foreign bills on London but drew relatively few bills on abroad. Because the predominant mass of exchange dealings took place on the foreign side, the exchange rate between England and most other countries was fixed abroad—not in London. This made the foreign trader acutely sensitive to exchange fluctuations while the British trader, selling in sterling and receiving London bills, was comparatively indifferent.

### 依据与细节

　　The author states the principle: when two countries trade with each other in equal amounts, only one set of bills is necessary. If the foreign exporter draws on London for the full value of his exports, those bills are sent as remittances to pay the British exporter; conversely, if the British exporter draws, the other side need not draw.

　　In practice, "bills drawn by Great Britain on abroad are vastly outnumbered by those drawn from abroad on London." Contributing causes included:
- The magnitude of British trade;
- Currency stability and the certainty that "a bill on London means gold and nothing but gold";
- Ease of obtaining credit for those who deserved it;
- Freedom from invasion.

　　Consequences:
- London became "to a great extent the settling-place of Europe and the world";
- Foreign sellers preferred to draw on London for ready money and speedy capital turnover;
- Foreign importers preferred remitting to London over being drawn upon, because the rate depended on their own bargaining rather than on a London bill-broker;
- British traders saw exchange-rate fluctuations as "but of academic interest" because they sold in sterling; they cared about the **course of discount** because they might have to discount paper before payday.

　　The author lists directional control: England–United States rates fixed in New York; England–Brazil in Rio; England–Turkey in Constantinople; with **India** as the most notable exception.

### 限制与边界

　　原文未提及： the volume ratio of British-drawn to foreign-drawn bills; how the India exception operated in practice; whether the analysis held for commodities priced in sterling versus local currencies; the time period over which the cited stability was observed.

### 原文摘录

> "London has become to a great extent the settling-place of Europe and the world, and the seller, wherever he may be, of a good bill on London can always be sure of finding a buyer and of realizing a fair price."
> "The rate of exchange between two countries ... must be fixed by the one that draws and negotiates the bill."
> "The real significance of the fluctuations can only be grasped by viewing them from the foreign [e. g., American] standpoint."
