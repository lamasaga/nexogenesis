### 核心表达

　　State banking laws set minimum capital requirements for state banks that varied by region and were usually graded by city population, while trust companies were generally required to have substantially larger minimum capitals, though their requirements were often set by special charter rather than general law.

### 依据与细节

　　- Eastern States and the more easterly Middle Western States generally required state banks to have at least $25,000 capital (one exception noted but not named).
　　- In other sections, most States allowed incorporation with capital as small as $10,000; a few set minima of $15,000, $20,000, $25,000, or $30,000; North Carolina permitted $5,000.
　　- In 29 of 37 States and Territories requiring a specified capital for state banks under general law, the amount was graded according to population; the grading was intended to adjust each bank's capital to the volume of its business, using city size as a rough index.
　　- Minute gradation in many laws was designed to encourage bank formation in smaller cities and towns, with fewer grades for larger places.
　　- The national-bank act provisions on capital payment and surplus served as models for many States.
　　- Several States made no provision for trust-company capital; Connecticut, Delaware, New Hampshire, and Vermont incorporated trust companies only by special acts, with capital fixed by the legislature; Rhode Island incorporated them through a board that fixed terms including capital.
　　- Early general trust-company laws required much larger capital than banks, but later legislation showed a tendency to lower requirements.
　　- Trust-company minimum capital ranged from $5,000 in North Carolina to $1,000,000 in the District of Columbia; the majority of States requiring a specified minimum did not permit organisation below $100,000.
　　- In only one State (Iowa) was the minimum capital lower for trust companies than for state banks; in six States it was the same; in all others it was larger.
　　- Accumulation of surplus was required in fewer States for trust companies than for banks.

### 限制与边界

　　- The text does not state how often actual capital exceeded minima, or whether minimum-capital rules were effectively enforced.
　　- No comparison is made between capital adequacy and failure rates.

### 原文摘录

> The capital stock is a buffer interposed between the bank's creditors and losses which the bank may suffer. If there is no capital, losses may fall directly on the creditor, and the larger the capital stock, other things being equal, the less the likelihood of loss to the depositor.
> The first general laws for the incorporation of trust companies in the United States required such companies to have a much larger capital than that required for banks, but the later legislation shows a distinct tendency in the direction of lowering the requirements in regard to capital.
